Sejung Yoon /SEH-jung YOON/

Political Sociology · Political Polarization · Social Networks · Political Participation · Computational Social Science

I am a sociologist studying the relational foundations of political engagement: how ties to other people, groups, and organizations draw individuals into public life, and what happens to engagement when those ties are strained by division and distrust. My research has examined how emotional ties and social networks draw people into political action and sustain solidarity across movements. Building on this work, I ask when political differences make everyday conversation too costly to continue, and why declining trust in institutions leads some people to withdraw while drawing others into new forms of action.

For eight years, I taught Korean language and composition to middle and high school students. Outside of research, I enjoy running, working out, and reading Korean novels.

Research

Research Areas

When Political Difference Becomes Distance

How does political polarization seep into everyday relationships and reshape how people interact? Perceived political differences can change whether people are willing to talk with one another at all. I ask when political disagreement closes off conversation among relatives, friends, and coworkers, and when ongoing ties sustain communication across it. In related work, I examine how partisanship conditions the link between feelings toward social groups and political action, and use computational analysis of online communities to trace how misogynistic slurs broadened into generalized stigma markers.

How Distrust Redirects Participation

Declining trust in public institutions is often read as a path to civic withdrawal. Yet distrust does not lead everyone away from public life; for some citizens, it redirects participation toward alternative forms of action. We still know little about how the distribution of trust across institutions, organizations, and personal relationships shapes these diverging paths, or how civic organizations mobilize support when their own credibility is uncertain. I plan to pursue these questions through survey analysis of trust configurations and text analysis of nonprofit crowdfunding campaigns.

How Relationships Shape Collective Action

Solidarity is often understood as a product of shared identity or common interests. Yet people also act on behalf of groups they do not belong to, and movements with different agendas still find ways to work together. My research asks how social networks make this possible. I have examined how emotional closeness toward social minorities is linked to political participation, particularly among members of voluntary associations, and how activists build coalitions across labor, feminist, and climate movements. I am now examining how civic participation is reproduced across civil society as a whole, through focus groups with insiders, boundary spanners, and outsiders of established organizations.
